DATA ISLAND

Forward:

“[Technology] ‘is the active human interface with the material world.” -Ursula Le Guin

Technology experts use the term “data island” to describe data stores (collections of information) that, for security reasons, are intentionally hard to access. They are metaphorical islands—isolated land masses so to speak, surrounded by water. Data islands can be problematic when the information they hold cannot be accessed by the people seeking it. For many, my duel interests in furniture design and computation might seem like data islands—two completely unrelated and siloed fields that are hard to intellectually access. This project is an attempt to bridge these two apparent “data islands” and show just how harmonious they can be. In order to understand the connections between them, it is useful to describe my journey at RISD and how my twin passions for craft and technology organically evolved.

My time studying furniture design at RISD was interrupted by the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic. During that period, I started to blend together tools and styles in unexpected ways as I tried to access scraps of history and “how-tos” online. Then, in the first semester of my sophomore-year, furniture design classes, we were given a box of hand tools and allowed to use minimal power tools to construct a table using wooden joinery. It seemed like we were transported back to a time long ago. Or to an idea of a time long ago. Simultaneously, I began to foster a love of hand-coding websites as I came to realize that evidence of all my work (and indeed self) may at some point be accessed only digitally. In both these processes, I was going backwards in time to utilize tools that predated me in order to fabricate and mimic histories, and better understand my present day. This time marked a pivotal moment for me in how I interfaced with the world. Afterwards, I doubled down on learning traditional craft as it fed some part of my soul that had never been fulfilled through other means. I took computer science classes at Brown University which scratched an itch in my brain to understand the most powerful and widely used tools of our contemporary world. In both three-dimensional and bit-based worlds, I was hitting “right click > inspect” to discover how things were built. Learning coding and woodworking gave me confidence and empowerment. They were complimentary (yet rarely combined skills) that I could lean into when I felt insecure. By learning how things were built, I felt a little more in control. But the two interests very quickly proved to be more than just a means to make me feel calm. They revealed themselves as” twin flame” interests and came together to inform my work as a furniture designer. It began with a wifi router box made of birch bark. It then grew into a love of collecting old computers, then creating home office furniture that combined traditional woodworking techniques like green woodworking with the history of vanities and home computing. And why not? With more people working from home than ever, we are reinventing what it means to work online. New ideas of work need new styles and tools. As a furniture designer, I’ve enjoyed combining my love of traditional craft and computation in my work and bridging these two “data islands” in a harmonious way.

Still, intellectually, I felt that there was something missing in this pursuit. As I learned more about coding and craft, I felt that it was not only their futures that could be better combined, but also their pasts. The root of the English word “technology” is “techno” (Latin for “craft” or “art”). At the beginning of “technology” are our earliest building tools. Jump to the industrial revolution and the word “craft” came into popular use as a way to describe the difference between labor done by man and machine. This places the birth of modern computation in the middle of craft and industry with the invention of the jacquard loom. The same type of punch card systems used to operate the loom were used to give instructions to the earliest computers. Between craft and industrial production we find the grandmother of modern computation–the jacquard loom. After this point, craftwork and computation converged and disbanded… or did they?

As someone who was becoming deeply interested in both the history and theory of craft and computation, I wondered if there were ways of looking at the history of computation through a craft lens, and if the language of traditional crafts could be brought into computational critique. After all, computers are scary and hard to think about. But everyone can understand a chair. I wanted to bring approachability to new technology through craft.

While studying computer science at Brown University, I would commonly tell people that my studies in fine woodworking–especially in my most rigorous year of instruction during my sophomore year at RISD – provided me with the skills I felt I needed to be a good coder. Furthermore, I would tell people that I think of coding in the same way I think of woodworking, as the two had a lot of the same considerations. This surprised people. So I began to try to describe the similarities. And as I started to write stories about my own experiences woodworking and coding, I was finding new bridges between old and new technologies. I discovered that most people don’t know what green woodworking is, or really what the process of building fine furniture looks like, so I also started to make videos of myself at work as a way to help communicate what I do.

As I started to record myself woodworking, I also began to watch myself woodworking. Like hearing yourself talk, seeing myself fumble around with axes and other hand tools made me realize how out of place I really did seem in shop environments. In order to feel more confident working in the woodshop, I had put a lot of effort into what I wore and I had deliberately chosen clothing that made me feel glamorous. Because duh, that's what so many of us do when we need to feel good about ourselves, more confident. (But even I admit that wearing a second hand fur coat may have, in retrospect, been a bit much.) Recording myself at work was a portal into a world I had never been fully aware of before, and I began to wonder who this person on the screen could become. I have always wanted to write a series of essays on craft and computation and how they have helped me. But by watching myself in those videos, seeing the strange office furniture I make and the jumble of ideas I possess, I was made aware that all of it could exist outside of myself in the form of narrative fiction. By moving out of reality and into the genre of fiction, perhaps I could better explain how craft and computation are linked, how woodworking can be a helpful skill for coding and how the future of personal computing would benefit by looking to the chair-makers. But perhaps most importantly, I could have more fun.

So having said all this, let me also say that this is a romantic sci-fi novel that speaks to the joys of woodworking and tinkering with computers in the pursuit of love and self-discovery. Through disparate technologies, my two lovely ladies find some resolution in their fractured lives as they try to understand a path forward (when forward is yet to be defined). When the past and the future blur, fold and become fractured, we have the stories that give us purpose and they can be told through words, woodworking, or lines of code.

-Sarah Holloway

Love the Reeling Midnight Through

I followed the brick-lined sidewalk back to my apartment. On either side of a pothole ridden road the beech trees have, with their roots, pushed the sidewalk up closer to the sky. Many of the bricks are dislodged leaving cavities of gravel where they once sat. The little brick roads are charming the first few times you walk them but as the gravel tears at the rubber of my shoes, they become less enchanting. It's not easy living in a town best suited for boots when you only wear heels.

I was walking home in the early morning, after having been out all night. I wasn’t expecting to stay out or to have fallen asleep in a hybrid space that seemed both like a forgotten church and an emerging gallery. Then again, I had never imagined myself being in this situation. The night had sounded more promising than it had turned out. In the end, the evening played out almost like a default setting, as if I had become a Non-Playable-Character in my own game of life. I had recently been catching the beginnings of what I believe to be the onset of ennui, a loss in faith that there is any hope for joy or excitement or success. I felt static, and no escapist adventure after dark was going to pull me out of this feeling. There was some lack of connection happening that I couldn’t quite put my finger on. And the previous night was just a symptom of it.

The night began as I had just gotten back from my shift at the library where I spent the day transferring index cards into a computer system. I’m not sure how successful this undertaking will be, nor do I understand why the collection needs to be transferred. And for what purpose when the system will only be used by those who are making it. The computers will die and have to be replaced faster than paper. It's a bit ironic that I, the girl studying computational linguistics, is thinking like a luddite. After I finished my shift, on my walk home, I detoured to the drugstore and that is when I ran into an old roommate of mine, Mark. He asked me to come out with some of “our old friends”, the people who would drift in and out of our house while I was living there as a sublet tenant. It was kind he called them “our friends”, but I was always a stranger to them emerging from my room to have a glass of wine before disappearing back to where I came. I was exhausted from the day but he seemed so excited to have seen me and I thought that maybe going out would give me a boost of energy.

Even as I was walking to the bar, I questioned why I was going. Deep down I knew how the night would actually go. I would give a short but not short enough explanation about what I was working on for my masters. Eyes would begin to glaze over. God forbid I would get a follow-up question like “What’s computational linguistics?” Or the more frequent comment, “I thought you didn’t even own a computer!” Inevitably Mark would make a pass at me, as he always did when we were living together. I’ll stay for a drink, I thought, and then go home.

I got there as everyone seemed to be trickling out. By the time I returned with my red wine only four people remained, and almost as soon as I sat down, they all got up to leave. Only Mark and I were left. After catching up, we began to walk home and Mark suggested we go look at the new studio space he rented. I couldn’t think of a reason why not. I knew what the invite meant. Mark is a nice guy. He’s a golden-retriever-type. A tall and lanky skater boy, he’s always running around, always off to meet someone to go somewhere for a weekend to find a new skate spot. But he was someone who lived in the now, while I am perpetually stuck reflecting backwards and forwards. Whatever interest he has in me is only superficial as I know I would drive him crazy.

We got to the entrance of the old church and dipped into the doorway and past a large stone staircase leading to a makeshift storage space. He grabbed my waist and led me into the studio space. The ceiling was painted to look like the sky. Stars were painted in spackles. The filled-in cracks along the walls looked like morning glory vines climbing up to the ceiling. As I turned to look at the altar, he leaned in for a kiss and proceeded to stick his tongue down my throat. I played along. It seemed harmless in the moment.

As the morning fog rolled in, I found myself waking up on the hardwood floor. He had suggested we stay and not really wanting to walk home, I agreed. We had pushed together some of the velvet pew cushions and had fallen asleep on our makeshift bed without talking. I spent most of my night squished between two of the cushions that were too narrow to fit on one but too big to properly position myself on two. After counting the spackle stars, I fell lightly asleep. But when a hint of light filtered through the windows, I knew I needed to leave.

Any attraction I remotely felt was fleeting with Mark. I got up and as I was changing, I looked around the church, taking in my surroundings one last time. The sun was beginning to light up the church, allowing me to get a better sense of the space. The altar had been caged off and turned into a storage unit for amps and other equipment. While the skeletal remains of the church made me feel like a sinner, the actual architecture of the space had been dramatically altered to fit its new purpose as a studio and event space. I walked over to get a better look at the equipment behind the cage. That's when I saw it for the first time, a computer sitting on a table near the back. It looked so friendly. No taller than a foot and a bit, its plastic exterior was boxy with a small square screen. You could fit two floppy disks in the port. Seeing it made me smile. Mark woke up and we made polite conversation, the type you would have with your family neighbor at the grocery store. We put everything back where we found it and then left the same way we entered. As we said goodbye, Mark suggested we do it again sometime. I agreed, but I knew that neither of us would be calling the other.

I walked the brick road back home following the slight elevation changes. I don’t really know how much longer I can take this city. I came here for love then as the relationship was ending, I turned to scholarship. I thought I would enjoy it. Studying the language of computers seemed so unique and interesting. I was the only person in the program who did not have a background in computer science. Rather naively, I thought that my lack of knowledge might provide me with a different perspective. But I have just felt out of place.

-Sally

The Hierophant

Cars pass me by. It's tourist season and I have the strong feeling I look out of place. I wonder if I should attempt to hitchhike the rest of the way to avoid the threat of a rain shower. Had my phone not been dead for the past several hours, I could have some comfort that I was walking in the right direction. I follow the white line at the edge of the asphalt. The road I walk along has a narrow shoulder that falls off three feet, distinguishing where the road has been elevated out of the forest. Ferns shelter the ground like an umbrella from the morning mist. Blue reflects off all surfaces, I think to play tricks on the eyes, to make everything look greener. It's a remarkable illusion: how the light refracts onto the forest to produce more shades of green than I can catalog in my mind. David Wagner is right, “If what a tree or a bush does is lost on you, You are surely lost.”

A red honda civic drives by. I sheepishly stick out my thumb to hitch a ride. It made me feel like a wannabe Vagabond. The car passes on. I don’t have it in me for more rejection. I’ll just walk if I can remember the way. I remember passing by an abandoned inn on the drive up to Julie’s the day before. Julie, a friend and old friend from New York had moved back to Vancouver, then back to where she grew up, on Salt Spring to start a family. She invited everyone from the mainland out to Salt Spring for a baby shower. I pass by the park where the Saturday farmers fill the harbourfront. Now deserted, twenty-four hours ago the space was buzzing with silversmiths, candle makers, and mead makers all at their booths selling what keeps them in Salt Spring. Not having much need for home goods I spent my time in the market getting my tarot read by a poet.

Lily and I, for a moment, peered into each other's lives. She with her cards and me with my phone. After she read my cards she handed me her business card which had enough information for me to connect her to numerous profiles and pages. I ruin the mystery of meeting a stranger. I can’t help but read back the decades of data the internet has on all of us. Still there is something these cards can show me that my internet searches cannot.

The last time I had my tarot read was just before I started dating my partner. Five years, a failed Visa application, and an impending break-up later, an old friend offered me a job working construction on the island. I spent every summer of college in Vancouver and when I landed it felt like I dropped into my life at my last save point, five years ago on new years day–nothing had changed. Nostalgia has been a good bandaid for my grieving heart but after a week or so back in Vancouver, the guilt and loneliness I felt in New York had found its way back to me. I think a summer on Salt Spring Island will be good for me. It's slower here, things are done “the good old ways”.

I finally arrived at the coffee shop, wet and with the beginnings of a caffeine headache. The coffee shop is decorated like a chain even though there's only one. A small detail I find amusing. There is a battle between the cool glow coming in through the windows and the yellow overhead lights. I opt to sit where the lighting is warmest, somewhat in the middle of the coffee shop. My phone lights up and I am once again flooded with blue light.

It appears that since I have been offline a wifi router box of mine has gone viral. Ironically since my absence from the Lotex Corp, an artist collective, has been gaining attention for our “avant garde use of low-tech craft techniques within daily design objects”. Many of the objects we made over the years have been circling sub groups on the clear net and finding their way into small niche channels. The box I made had wifi routing parts mixed with birch box-making techniques. There was something that attracted me to these vessels of necessity. Birch bark is one of my favorite materials to work with as it's the perfect in-between working with fabric and wood. I punctured the birch with an awl to create holes to weave wax linen thread between, connecting it to the wooden box connecting me to the larger world. It was a solution to my lack of space and access to materials in New York and the first thing I made that made me think I had a purpose and maybe a reason to be creating objects. The attention of being seen on the clear net is meaningless at best. I will get alerts from my phone to answer some questions for publications probably run by 16-year olds. It's so unclear to know what is useful and what is screaming into a void. At least here on the Island the people I talk to have to talk back. I know I’m retreating to this island but I haven't given up. I was told that at the top of the mountain there is a grove a birch trees and its almost the yearly harvest. The box was the thing that first pushed me to believe in myself and maybe working with birch bark again can help me find that part of myself again.

When I left the party was still going I wonder upon my arrival back if everyone will have gone to bed. I must say I’ve never met someone who celebrates birth so hard.

-N.

The Macintosh

I haven't seen Mark in months. I think he went to California to avoid the coming cold months. Like I said, I don’t really care. That had been the first time I had seen him in almost a year but a wave when we walked by wouldn’t kill him. I never for a second thought that there was a spark between me and mark but still that night is burned in my memory.

In class we were asked to bring in a piece of technology that was impactful to you. And to be honest the only machine I have ever felt a strong connection to is one I have never used. That little Macintosh made me smile like it and I was in on a little secret. (That I was a slut. No.) That in that same place we were only instruments for others' delight.

About a week ago my friend Siobhan mentioned there was going to be a masquerade party in the church. Perfect timing. I had a mission at this party–to take the computer. I can’t decide If I am relieved to finally have felt a sense of excitement or If I should be in tears over the idea that the only thing in my life giving me the excitement is made up of copper wires wrapped in injection molded plastic. I can’t help but want to believe that while my one night stand may not have been the most romantic or exciting thing in the world, it led me to something I needed.

-Sally
